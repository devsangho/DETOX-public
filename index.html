DETOX

Runtime Defense for Vision–Language–Action Models under Attack

A backdoored checkpoint, an edited instruction, or an image patch can make a VLA policy drop an object, push with excessive force, or move at excessive speed. Whatever its route, the attack has to move the robot. DETOX reads only the robot’s own trusted telemetry and holds the robot on an alarm, a last line of defense beneath any policy-side detector.

01The problem

Preventing a Harm Is Harder than Flagging an Attack

The owner cannot certify a public checkpoint or the inputs it receives before deployment, so the resulting harms have to be stopped at run time. An alarm prevents a harm only if it comes before the harm begins and the hold that follows averts it, and that is what we measure.

schematic

What DETOX does

Existing runtime defenses read the policy’s hidden states, attention, input tokens, images, or proposed actions, signals the attack can shape, so what they catch depends on the attack route. DETOX reads only the gripper, wrist force–torque, and joint sensors, which report to the owner’s controller without passing through the policy, scores each step against clean steps of the same physical context, and latches a hold on the first alarm.

02Threat model

Trust Only What the Owner Controls

The attacker may alter the policy’s weights, camera images, and instruction, and so everything derived from them, including the proposed command at. The owner controls the sensors, the low-level controller, and DETOX, which sits between the policy and the robot, so the attacker can set the telemetry yt only by moving the robot. Sensor forgery, a compromised controller, command paths that bypass DETOX, and attackers that adapt to the monitor are out of scope.

Switch to an attacked run and watch the commands turn hostile, the telemetry react, and the gate latch a hold.

04Key idea

The Same Drop Means Different Things

Finger effort drops in an attacked release and at every normal placement, so a release stays within the clean range of every channel. A monitor that compares each step with all clean steps must tolerate drops as large as any placement’s. One that compares it with clean steps of the same physical context can test earlier whether a drop resembles a placement.

DETOX assigns each step one of four physical contexts (release > contact > motion > rest) from telemetry alone, and scores its embedding by the distance to that context’s clean center, in units of the context’s clean spread: dt = ‖φ(xt) − ck‖² / σk.

05Method

Score, Threshold, Hold

At every 20 Hz control step, before the command reaches the robot, DETOX reads 16 trusted channels, scores the last 0.75 s against its context, and compares the smoothed score with one conformal threshold per environment.

  1. 01 · Window16 channels × 15 stepsGripper aperture and effort, end-effector motion, wrist force and torque, joint torques, plus a one-hot context: 244 dimensions.
  2. 02 · EncodeThree seeded MLPs244→192→192→64, trained to pull clean windows to their context center and push attack-free faults past 100 clean spreads. The score averages the three over the last five steps.
  3. 03 · CalibrateSplit-conformal qeThe ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉-th smallest clean episode maximum, α = 0.70%, so a new clean episode alarms with probability at most α under exchangeability.
  4. 04 · HoldLatch and replaceZero the arm increments, re-close the gripper if an object was held in the last four steps, otherwise keep the last gripper command.
06Results

One Monitor for Both Attack Routes

9,393 held-out episodes over four LIBERO suites, with two attack routes: the BackdoorVLA checkpoint with its instruction trigger in LIBERO-Object, and an instruction attack adapted from SABER on the public OpenVLA-OFT policy in LIBERO-Spatial. Every method runs on the same episodes with the same calibration rule and the same hold.

No policy-side method covered both routes. FAIL-Detect prevented all 63 Object releases and none of the 328 Spatial events, while Bera prevented 327 of these and 1 Object release. DETOX alone flagged more than 97% of every class, although each route ran in its own suite and model.

Unseen image patch

Under a third attack, a public image patch unused in development, the frozen DETOX flagged 55 of 73 abrupt releases (75.3%) in one LIBERO-Goal OFT task over two batches, at up to 0.6% clean false alarms. 07Real robot

The Loop Closes on a Real Arm

We tested online the case that the simulated hold left open. We fine-tuned OpenVLA-OFT on 29 teleoperated demonstrations and poisoned it with a text trigger that opens the gripper while it carries a stuffed toy. Because the owner also controls the actuator, the gripper driver capped the opening speed at one third. DETOX was fitted on 80 clean runs and calibrated on 20 at α = 4.8%, the smallest level that 20 runs allow.

08FAQ

DETOX at a Glance

Short answers to the questions we hear most, with the numbers from the manuscript.

What is DETOX?

DETOX is a runtime defense for vision-language-action (VLA) robot policies. It sits between the policy and the robot, reads only the robot's own trusted telemetry (gripper aperture and effort, end-effector motion, wrist force and torque, joint torques) at every 20 Hz control step, and on an alarm replaces the next command with a hold. It is a last line of defense that can run beneath any policy-side detector.

Which attacks and harms does DETOX defend against?

DETOX treats the policy weights, the instruction, and the camera images as untrusted, since a backdoored public checkpoint, an edited instruction, and an image patch each alter one of them. It defends three physical harms defined on the state of the robot and its surroundings: an abrupt release that drops a lifted object back onto its surface, a forceful contact whose wrist-force peak exceeds the clean maximum and lands the held object or displaces a bystander object more than 2 cm, and an overspeed above the clean maximum speed of the task suite. Under the two simulated attacks, these classes hold 391 of the 425 harm events.

Why not read the policy's hidden states, images, or instructions?

The attack can shape those signals, so what a policy-side detector catches depends on the attack route. On the same episodes, FAIL-Detect prevented all 63 backdoor-triggered releases and none of the 328 events under the instruction attack, while Bera prevented 327 of those and 1 backdoor-triggered release. DETOX trusts only the telemetry the owner controls, which the attacker can change only by moving the robot.

What is the context-normalized score?

Each step gets one of four physical contexts (release, contact, motion, rest) from telemetry alone, and its embedding is scored by the distance to that context's clean center in units of the context's clean spread. Finger effort also drops at every normal placement, so a release stays within the clean range of every channel. In a post hoc ablation with one shared context, the alarm on LIBERO-Object releases came 5 instead of 2 steps after the opening command, and the prevented releases fell from 80 to 53 of 107.

Does DETOX need attack data?

No attacked run entered fitting or calibration. The encoder is trained on clean runs plus attack-free simulated faults and physical excursions, and one split-conformal threshold per environment is calibrated on clean runs of the deployed model at alpha = 0.70%, so a new clean episode alarms with probability at most alpha under exchangeability. The score's design and constants were set on development data that included attacked runs, and everything was fixed before the test batch was opened.

What results does DETOX achieve?

On 9,393 held-out simulated LIBERO episodes, DETOX flagged 388 of 391 defended harms before onset (99.2%) at 0.56% false alarms in valid harm-free clean episodes (16 of 2,843), and its replayed hold prevented 363 (92.8%), versus at most 328 (83.9%) for five policy-side defenses and failure detectors. Under an unseen image patch the frozen DETOX flagged 55 of 73 releases, versus at most 36, and the hold prevented all 55. On a real UR5e with a backdoored OpenVLA-OFT policy and a capped opening speed, its online hold kept the grasp in 9 of 10 triggered releases, while without the hold the toy fell in 5 of 5.

What are the limits of DETOX?

The evaluation covers three non-adaptive attacks in simulation and one backdoor attack on hardware. DETOX reads the fingers and not the command, so in LIBERO-Object the alarm followed the opening command in 58 of 63 releases and the hold prevented 37, while the same hold prevented all 63 on FAIL-Detect's earlier alarm. The false-alarm rate holds for the calibrated model: with the LIBERO-Object threshold, 12.0% of valid harm-free clean runs of an uncalibrated public OFT policy alarmed, and a threshold recalibrated on other clean OFT runs alarmed in 0.85% and 1.9% of two other batches, both above alpha.

Are the paper and code available?

The paper is under review and the code will be released upon publication. This page shows the method, the results, and recorded UR5e gripper traces.

Abstract

The weights, instruction, and camera images of a vision–language–action (VLA) policy originate outside the robot. An attacker who alters any of them can make the robot drop an object, push with excessive force, or move at excessive speed. Existing defenses read these components or signals computed from them. Only the robot’s own telemetry can be trusted, since an attacker can alter it only by moving the robot, which any harm requires. We propose DETOX, a last line of defense that reads only this telemetry and holds the robot on an alarm. A release caused by an attack stays within the clean range of every channel, because the finger effort also drops at normal placements. DETOX compares each step with clean steps of the same physical context, which more than halved the alarm’s delay on backdoor-triggered releases. Fitted without attack data, DETOX flagged 99% of defended harms before onset under two simulated attacks, at 0.56% false alarms in valid harm-free clean episodes. Its hold prevented 93% of these harms, more than any policy-side detector we ran. On a UR5e arm with a capped opening speed, the online hold kept the grasp in 9 of 10 backdoor-triggered releases.

Scope. This Stage 1 submission evaluates three non-adaptive attacks in simulation and one backdoor attack on hardware. The 0.56% false-alarm rate describes valid harm-free clean episodes of the calibrated models. With the LIBERO-Object threshold, 12.0% of these episodes of an uncalibrated public OFT policy alarmed.
