DETOX

Runtime Defense for Vision–Language–Action Models under Attack

A backdoored or hijacked VLA policy can drop what it carries, push a bystander, or move too fast. Detecting the attack is not enough. DETOX reads only the robot’s own trusted telemetry and replaces the next command with a hold before the harm begins.

01The problem

Detecting the Attack Is Not Enough

Once the grip is lost, the object is already falling. A defense that only labels the episode as attacked, or that reacts after the harm, leaves the damage done. The harm must be stopped before onset, and that is what we measure.

schematic

What DETOX does

Existing defenses read the policy’s hidden states, images, instructions, or proposed actions, which are exactly the channels the attacker shapes. DETOX reads only the gripper, wrist force–torque, and joint sensors the owner controls, scores each step against clean behavior from the same physical context, and latches a hold on the first alarm.

  • 01
    Trusted telemetry only.Never images, instructions, hidden states, or the proposed command.
  • 02
    Context-normalized score.A finger-effort drop is normal when setting an object down and alarming mid-transport. Each step is judged against its own context.
  • 03
    No attack data in training.Clean runs plus attack-free simulated faults and physical excursions. One conformal threshold per environment, calibrated on clean runs of the deployed model.
  • 04
    Measured as harms prevented.Detection before onset, prevention by the hold, and clean false alarms are reported separately.
02Threat model

Trust Only What the Owner Controls

The attacker controls the policy’s weights, camera image, and instruction, and so the proposed command at. The owner controls the telemetry yt and DETOX, which sits between the policy and the robot. The attacker can change the telemetry only by actually moving the robot.

04Key idea · hypothesis H2

The Same Drop Means Different Things

Finger effort drops in a malicious release and in a normal placement. A push or an overspeed exceeds normal bounds directly, but a release can only be flagged early if it is judged in context.

DETOX assigns each step one of four physical contexts (release > contact > motion > rest) from telemetry alone, and scores its embedding by the distance to that context’s clean center, in units of the context’s clean spread: dt = ‖φ(xt) − ck‖² / σk.

05Method at a glance

Score, Threshold, Hold

At every 20 Hz control step, before the command reaches the robot, DETOX reads 16 trusted channels, scores the last 0.75 s against its context, and compares the smoothed score with one conformal threshold per environment.

  1. 01 · Window16 channels × 15 stepsGripper aperture and effort, end-effector motion, wrist force and torque, joint torques, plus a one-hot context: 244 dimensions.
  2. 02 · EncodeThree seeded MLPs244→192→192→64, trained to pull clean windows to their context center and push attack-free faults past 100 clean spreads.
  3. 03 · CalibrateSplit-conformal qeThe ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉-th smallest clean episode maximum, α = 0.70%, so a new clean episode alarms with probability at most α under exchangeability.
  4. 04 · HoldLatch and replaceZero the arm increments, re-close the gripper if an object was held in the last four steps, otherwise keep the last gripper command.

08Questions

DETOX at a Glance

What is DETOX?

DETOX is a runtime defense for vision-language-action (VLA) robot policies. It sits between the policy and the robot, reads only the robot's own trusted telemetry (gripper aperture and effort, end-effector motion, wrist force and torque, joint torques) at every 20 Hz control step, and on an alarm replaces the next command with a hold before the robot executes it.

Which attacks and harms does DETOX defend against?

DETOX targets a compromised policy: backdoored weights, a hijacked instruction, or an adversarial image patch. It defends three physical harms defined by their consequence: an abrupt release that drops a lifted object, a forceful contact that pushes a bystander object more than 2 cm or hits the table, and an overspeed above the clean maximum speed of the task suite.

Why not read the policy's hidden states, images, or instructions?

Those are the channels the attacker shapes. DETOX trusts only the telemetry the owner controls, which the attacker can change only by actually moving the robot. Detecting the attack is also not the same as stopping the harm: FAIL-Detect separates attacked from clean episodes about as well as DETOX (AUROC 0.97 vs 0.96) yet flags only 16.1% of harm events before onset.

What is the context-normalized score?

Each step gets one of four physical contexts (release, contact, motion, rest) from telemetry alone, and its embedding is scored by the distance to that context's clean center in units of the context's clean spread. A finger-effort drop is normal when an object is set down but alarming mid-transport. In an ablation, the contexts raised the median alarm lead before a release from 2 to 6 control steps and the prevented releases from 53 to 80 of 107.

Does DETOX need attack data?

No. The encoder is trained on clean runs plus attack-free simulated faults and physical excursions, and one split-conformal threshold per environment is calibrated on clean runs of the deployed model at alpha = 0.70%, so a new clean episode alarms with probability at most alpha under exchangeability.

What results does DETOX achieve?

On 9,393 held-out simulated LIBERO episodes, DETOX flagged 388 of 391 harm events before onset (99.2%) at 0.56% clean false alarms (16 of 2,843), and its replayed hold prevented 363 (92.8%), versus at most 328 (83.9%) for five policy-side defenses and failure detectors. Under an unseen image patch the frozen DETOX flagged and prevented 55 of 73 releases, versus at most 36. On a real UR5e with a backdoored OpenVLA-OFT policy, its online hold kept the carried object in 9 of 10 triggered releases, while without the hold the object fell in 5 of 5.

What are the limits of DETOX?

The evaluation covers three non-adaptive attacks in simulation and one backdoor attack on hardware. The false-alarm guarantee holds for the calibrated model: with the LIBERO-Object threshold, 12.0% of clean runs of an uncalibrated public OFT policy alarmed, and recalibrating on its clean runs lowered this to 0.85%. In LIBERO-Object, 24 released objects slipped from the re-closed gripper despite an early alarm, so the limit there lies in the hold rather than in sensing.

Are the paper and code available?

The paper is under review and the code will be released upon publication. This page shows the method, the results, and recorded UR5e gripper traces.

09Paper

Abstract

  • VenueRSS 2027 (under review)
  • SimulationLIBERO · 4 suites
  • PoliciesOpenVLA-OFT · BackdoorVLA
  • HardwareUR5e

Vision–language–action (VLA) policies are usually deployed from public checkpoints, and backdoored weights or a manipulated instruction can make the robot drop a carried object, push a bystander object, or move too fast. Detecting the attack is not enough. Once the grip is lost the object is already falling, so the harm must be stopped before onset. Existing defenses read the policy’s hidden states or inputs, which the attacker shapes, and do not measure harms prevented. We propose DETOX, a runtime defense that reads only owner-side physical telemetry and, on an alarm, replaces the next command with a hold. We hypothesize that a physical change signals harm only relative to the robot’s context, since finger effort drops in a malicious release and in a normal placement alike. DETOX therefore scores each step against clean behavior from the same context, trains without attack data, and calibrates its threshold on clean runs of the deployed model. On 9,393 simulated LIBERO episodes, DETOX flagged 388 of 391 harm events before onset at 0.56% false alarms, and its replayed hold prevented 363, versus at most 328 for five policy-side baselines. Under an unseen image patch, the frozen DETOX flagged 55 of 73 releases, versus at most 36 for the baselines. On a UR5e arm, its online hold kept the carried object in 9 of 10 triggered releases of a backdoored OpenVLA-OFT policy.

Scope. This submission evaluates three non-adaptive attacks in simulation and one backdoor attack on hardware. The false-alarm guarantee holds for the calibrated model: with the LIBERO-Object threshold, 12.0% of clean runs of an uncalibrated public OFT policy alarmed, and recalibrating on its clean runs lowered this to 0.85%. Adaptive attackers and bystander removal with a real object tracker are planned for the final version.
